# Argument Map Template

## Overview

The Argument Map is the **logical skeleton** of your section. It comes BEFORE the Narrative Outline and forces you to state WHY each paragraph is logically necessary, not just topically relevant.

Each paragraph must pass the **Kill Test**: if removed, what breaks? If nothing breaks, cut it.

---

## The Argument Map Table

Use this template for each section of your manuscript:

```markdown
## Argument Map: [Section Name]

| # | Claim | Evidence | WHY HERE | READER'S LIVE QUESTION | Reader State After | Kill Test | Shape |
|---|-------|----------|----------|------------------------|-------------------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | [what this paragraph argues] | [what supports it] | [logical connection to previous] | [the single question the reader is asking here] | [what reader now knows/believes] | [what breaks if removed?] | [paragraph shape] |
| 2 | [claim] | [evidence] | [why logically necessary] | [live question] | [reader state] | [kill test] | [shape] |
| 3 | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
```

---

## The Logical Chain

Before filling the table, state the logical flow of your argument:

```
Logical chain: P1 establishes X → P2 challenges X with Y → P3 resolves with Z → P4 extends to...
```

This forces you to see the **logical necessity** before you write. If you can't name the chain, the structure isn't tight enough.

---

## The Kill Test Column

**Critical column.** This directly addresses Bem's deletion test at the paragraph level.

**Rules:**
- If removing a paragraph breaks nothing, DELETE it.
- If it breaks something, EXPLICITLY STATE what breaks.
- The answer must be precise and causal, not vague.

**Example Kill Tests:**
- ✓ "Removing this breaks the reader's ability to understand why the counterargument (P4) is even relevant"
- ✓ "This paragraph establishes the mechanism; without it, the jump from evidence (P3) to theory (P5) is unsupported"
- ✓ "This contextualizes the limitation; without it, the reader might dismiss the finding"
- ✗ "This paragraph provides background" (too vague)
- ✗ "It's important context" (not causal)

---

## The WHY HERE Column

**Forces logical necessity.** This is where most writers fail.

### NOT Acceptable Answers

- "Because it's related to the topic"
- "Because this is where this topic goes"
- "Because the reader needs background"
- "For completeness"
- "It adds context"

These are topical proximity answers. They don't explain why THIS paragraph must come in THIS position in the logical chain.

### Acceptable Answers

- "Because P3 needs the reader to already know X from P2"
- "Because the counterargument in P4 only makes sense after the evidence in P3"
- "Because without this paragraph, the jump from P1 to P3 would feel like a non sequitur"
- "Because this mechanism directly explains why the pattern in P2 exists"
- "Because the reader needs to reject this objection before accepting the theory in P5"
- "Because the finding in P3 contradicts the expectation set up in P1"

These are **logical necessity** answers. They show WHY this paragraph must exist and WHEN (relative to other paragraphs).

---

## The READER'S LIVE QUESTION Column (Gopen 2004)

One field per planned paragraph:

**READER'S LIVE QUESTION: <the single question the reader is asking here>**

**The rule:** paragraph *k* answers question *k*, and its LAST sentence raises question *k+1*.
The reader always carries exactly one live question; each paragraph either answers it or loses them.

**Diagnostics:**
- If you cannot phrase the question in ONE sentence, the paragraph has no clear job — revisit WHY HERE.
- If the paragraph answers a question the reader is not yet asking, a setup paragraph is missing (or paragraphs are out of order).
- If the last sentence of paragraph k fails to raise question k+1, the chain breaks — the next paragraph reads as a non sequitur no matter how well it is written.
- Verify the handoffs pairwise down the whole map: the question raised at the end of k must be the question k+1 actually answers.

## The Shape Column

Every paragraph has a **shape** — a structural pattern that signals logic to the reader. Align the shape to your Intellectual Altitude and message.

### Six Shapes (Reference: writing-rules.md Level 3)

| Shape | Description | When to Use | Signal to Reader |
|-------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Claim-first** | State the claim upfront, then provide evidence | Main findings; direct conclusions | "Here's what I'm saying" |
| **Evidence-first** | Present evidence, then draw the claim | Complex findings; reader needs setup | "Here's what I found; here's what it means" |
| **Counterargument-first** | Present an objection, then refute it | Defending your position; anticipating skepticism | "You might think X, but..." |
| **Question-first** | Open with a question, then answer it | Generating curiosity; signal intellectual work | "This raises a question..." |
| **Narrative** | Tell a story or sequence of events | Qualitative findings; process or evolution | "Here's how it unfolded" |
| **Contrast** | Juxtapose two positions/findings, highlight the difference | Comparison sections; showing nuance | "Unlike X, this paper finds..." |

**Rule:** Your shape choice should match your message direction. If you're building certainty, use Claim-first. If you're building reasoning, use Evidence-first or Question-first.

## Relationship to Narrative Outline

**Argument Map = logical skeleton.**
**Narrative Outline = narrative wrapper.**

### The Flow

1. **Build Argument Map** (this template)
   - Define what each paragraph claims
   - State WHY it's logically necessary
   - Test with Kill Test
   - Choose a shape for each

2. **Build Narrative Outline**
   - Wrap the logical skeleton in narrative flow
   - Add transitions, signposting, story arc
   - Maintain the logical necessity from the Argument Map

3. **Write prose**
   - Fill in evidence, develop claims
   - Keep the logical chain visible
   - Never violate the WHY HERE reasoning

### Key Insight

A paragraph can have a strong logical position (WHY HERE is clear) but weak narrative shape (it feels abrupt or disconnected). The Narrative Outline bridges this gap — it makes the logic READABLE while preserving the logic itself.

**Example:**
- Argument Map says: "P2 establishes background because P3 depends on it"
- Narrative Outline adds: "To understand why X matters, the reader first needs Y. Here's Y..."
- Prose includes: transitions, examples, pacing that make the logic feel natural

---

## Before You Write: Checklist

- [ ] Logical chain is named and makes sense
- [ ] Every WHY HERE is a logical necessity answer, not a topical one
- [ ] Every paragraph has a one-sentence READER'S LIVE QUESTION; paragraph k answers question k and its last sentence raises question k+1 (Gopen 2004)
- [ ] Every Kill Test states what actually breaks, not just that it's "important"
- [ ] No two adjacent paragraphs have overlapping claims
- [ ] The sequence cannot be reordered without breaking the chain
- [ ] You've chosen a shape for each paragraph and can justify it
- [ ] You can read the Claim + WHY HERE + Kill Test columns and feel the argument's force

If any checkbox fails, revise the map before writing prose.
