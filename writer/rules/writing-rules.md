### FREQUENCY FLAGS — Common Words at AI-Diagnostic Frequencies

These words are individually legitimate. They become AI markers when CLUSTERED or used at higher-than-human frequency. Monitor, do not ban.

| Word | Human Frequency | AI Frequency Problem | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| across | Normal preposition | AI overuses as transition ("across domains," "across studies") | Max 2 per section; prefer "in," "among," "between" where equivalent |
| within | Normal preposition | AI overuses as pseudo-precise locator ("within the context of") | Max 2 per section; often deletable ("within this framework" -> "in this framework" -> just delete) |
| findings | Normal results noun | AI defaults to "findings" over "results" excessively | Vary with "results," "data," "evidence," "pattern" |
| potential / potentially | Normal adjective/noun | AI overuses as hedge ("potential implications," "potential limitations") | Max 1 per section; prefer "possible" or rewrite to remove the hedge |
| highlighting / highlights | Normal reporting verb | AI defaults to "highlights" over "shows," "suggests," "indicates" | Vary with "shows," "suggests," "indicates"; max 1 per section |
| specifically | Normal adverb | AI uses to create false precision ("specifically within the domain of") | Max 1 per section; often deletable |
| primarily | Normal adverb | AI overuses as qualifier ("primarily driven by") | Max 1 per section; "mainly," "mostly," "largely" are alternatives |
| predominantly | Normal adverb | Same pattern as "primarily" | Max 1 per section; "mostly," "largely" are alternatives |
| typically | Normal adverb | AI uses as generic hedge ("typically associated with") | Max 1 per section; "usually," "often," "generally" are alternatives |
| distinct / distinctive | Normal adjective | AI overuses for emphasis ("a distinct pattern") | Max 1 per section; "different," "separate," "clear" often work |
| remains | Normal verb | AI uses as stability marker ("remains unclear," "remains a challenge") | Max 1 per section; often rewritable ("is still unclear," "continues to be") |

### Kobak Corpus Reference (Science Advances, 2025)

**Source**: Kobak, Gonzalez-Marquez, Horvat, & Lause (2025). 15.1 million PubMed abstracts, 2010-2024. doi:10.1126/sciadv.adt3813
**Full word list**: `<SET_YOUR_PATH>/excess_words_kobak.csv` (899 words: 408 style, ~460 content, 24 mixed, 7 other) — build this CSV from the cited paper and point the lint gate at it.

**How the detection works** (know this so you can evade it):
1. For each word, expected 2024 frequency is computed by extrapolating from 2021-2022 trend (linear regression)
2. Two measures: **frequency gap** (delta = actual - expected) catches common words used MORE; **frequency ratio** (r = actual/expected) catches rare words used MUCH MORE
3. Excess threshold: delta > 0.01 OR r > max(3, 1/(8p))
4. Two independent, non-overlapping detection word sets:
   - **Rare set** (291 words, base rate p < 0.02): fully automatic, no manual curation. Estimated LLM share: 13.6%
   - **Common set** (10 words, manually optimized): across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within. Estimated LLM share: 13.4%
5. Both estimates converge at ~13.5%, validating each other
6. The method is UNBIASED: no classifier training needed, just corpus-level frequency comparison

**Key findings to internalize**:
- **66% of excess style words are VERBS**. The verb is where the AI fingerprint lives. Not nouns, not adjectives -- verbs.
- **COVID excess was content nouns** (vaccine, pandemic, lockdown). **LLM excess is style verbs** (enhancing, facilitating, showcasing). The KIND of excess distinguishes AI from topical trends.
- **Clustering is the kill shot**: 3+ marker words in one paragraph triggers detection even when each word is individually fine.
- **Top-tier journals show 7% LLM use; MDPI/Frontiers show 20-40%**. The detection bar is HIGHER at prestigious venues because the baseline is cleaner.
- **Native English speakers censor style words more effectively** than non-native speakers. Your job: write like a native speaker who would naturally avoid ornamental verbs.
- **The method improves over time**: as 2024 baselines accumulate, detection of 2025-2026 excess will be even more precise.

**Operational principle**: It is not enough to avoid banned words. You must use ALL words at human frequencies. A paragraph with "compelling," "substantial," and "predominantly" in it contains zero banned words but three Kobak soft-ban markers. That paragraph will be flagged.

### Zombie Nouns (Sword, 2012)

Nominalizations drain energy from prose. Convert back to verbs:
- "utilization" -> "use" (verb)
- "implementation" -> "implement"
- "examination" -> "examine"
- "facilitation" -> "help"
- "conceptualization" -> "conceive" or "frame"
- "operationalization" -> "define" or "measure"

**Detection rule**: Words ending in -tion, -ment, -ence, -ness, -ity that have a simpler verb form. Not all are bad (e.g., "frustration" as a construct name is fine). But "the examination of" is always worse than "we examined."

### Technical Term Consistency (Bem, 2003)

Once you name a construct, use that name every time. Synonyms confuse.
- If the scale is "workload strain," never switch to "job overload" or "role pressure"
- If the group is "app adopters," never switch to "mentoring-app participants"
- Repetition of key terms is a VIRTUE, not a flaw. Do not vary for variety's sake.

### Copula Rule

"Is" and "are" are fine. Never replace them with inflated alternatives:
- WRONG: "This finding serves as evidence that..."
- RIGHT: "This finding is evidence that..."
- WRONG: "AI stands as a complement to..."
- RIGHT: "AI is a complement to..."

### "Grounded in" / "Anchored in" Ban (Lesson 50)

- BAN: "grounded in" → replace with "consistent with", "based on", or "informed by"
- BAN: "anchored in" → replace with "consistent with", "based on", or "informed by"

## Level 2: Sentences

### Length Variance

Mix short and long sentences. Target: SD of sentence lengths > 8 words.
- Short (5-12 words): for emphasis, topic sentences, key claims
- Medium (15-25 words): for standard exposition
- Long (25-40 words): for complex ideas with clauses, conditions, qualifications

**BAD** (uniform ~20 words each):
> The results indicated that workload strain was associated with app use. This finding was consistent with our prediction. The effect remained significant after controlling for covariates.

**GOOD** (varied):
> Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use (OR = 1.48, p = .002). This held after controlling for age and years of experience, and the association reflected the cumulative burden across all nine task domains, with each domain contributing a small share.

### CV Measurement Targets (Measurable)

After writing any section, compute:
- Mean sentence length (in words)
- SD of sentence lengths
- CV = SD / Mean

Targets (based on empirical research):
- CV >= 0.40 (human range: 0.40-0.60; AI range: 0.25-0.35)
- No 4+ consecutive sentences within +/-3 words of each other
- At least one sentence under 8 words per section
- At least one sentence over 35 words per section
- SD > 8 words (existing rule; CV is an additional metric)

### Active Voice Default

- "We found" not "It was found"
- "The model showed" not "It was shown by the model"
- Passive is acceptable for general scientific statements ("Participants were recruited") and when the agent is irrelevant ("Data were collected in November 2024")

### Subject-Verb Proximity (Sword, 2012)

Keep subject and verb within 7 words of each other. Long insertions between subject and verb make sentences hard to parse.

- WRONG: "The association between overall workload strain, measured as the sum of all nine task-domain strain items, and the likelihood of adopting the mentoring app was significant."
- RIGHT: "Overall workload strain was significantly associated with mentoring-app use. We measured strain as the sum of all nine task-domain items."

### A4. Characters as Subjects (Williams, *Style*)

Sentences star actors as grammatical subjects performing verbs: the patients, the model, the clinicians, we. When nominalizations or abstractions hold the subject slot, readers must reconstruct who did what, and the prose goes bloodless.

**Flag rule:** any run of 2+ consecutive sentences whose grammatical subjects are nominalizations or abstractions gets rewritten so that at least one sentence returns an actor to the subject slot. Pairs with Zombie Nouns (Level 1): converting the nominalization back to a verb usually restores the actor for free.

**BEFORE** (three abstractions in a row as subjects):
> The utilization of the app by participants showed high engagement. The completion of the full onboarding occurred in 82% of cases. The perception of the tool as helpful was widespread.

**AFTER** (actors as subjects, performing verbs):
> Participants used the app heavily: 82% completed the full onboarding. Most described the tool as helpful.

### One Subclause Maximum

Each sentence gets one subordinate clause at most. Two or more embedded clauses create nesting that readers must mentally unpack.

- WRONG: "Although the agentic advantage, which was consistent across groups, was evident in the cluster analysis, which used three theoretically derived composites, the interaction was not significant."
- RIGHT: "The agentic advantage was consistent across both groups. This pattern emerged from a cluster analysis using three theoretically derived composites, though the Group x Cluster interaction was not significant."

### Banned Sentence Patterns

**No participial phrase endings:**
- BAN: "...marking a pivotal shift in the field"
- BAN: "...highlighting the importance of future research"
- BAN: "...suggesting the need for further investigation"

**No filler openings:**
- BAN: "It is important to note that..."
- BAN: "It is worth noting that..."
- BAN: "It should be noted that..."
- BAN: "It is interesting that..." (if interesting, show why; don't announce it)

**No throat-clearing (Bem, 2003):**
- BAN: "The results revealed that..." -> Just state the results
- BAN: "Analysis indicated that..." -> Just state what was found
- BAN: "An examination of the data showed..." -> State the finding

### Stats Placement: Finding First, Numbers in Parentheses

Lead with the finding in plain English, then put statistics in parentheses.

- WRONG: "A significant association was found (OR = 1.48, 95% CI [1.16, 1.89], p = .002) between workload strain and mentoring-app use."
- RIGHT: "Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use (OR = 1.48, 95% CI [1.16, 1.89], p = .002)."

### Grammar Precision (Bem, 2003; APA 7th)

- "compared with" not "compared to" (for statistical comparisons)
- "data are" not "data is" (data, criteria, phenomena are plural)
- "because" not "since" (when expressing cause; "since" = time only)
- "although" not "while" (when expressing contrast; "while" = time only)
- "that" for restrictive clauses; "which" for non-restrictive (APA reserves "which" for nonrestrictive only)
- "affect" (verb) vs. "effect" (noun, usually)
- "fewer" (countable) vs. "less" (continuous)
- "who" for humans; "that" for non-humans (APA 7th, Section 2.19)
- Do not use "would" to hedge: "it would appear that" -> "it appears that" (APA 7th, Section 2.14)
- Do not begin a sentence with a numeral or lowercase abbreviation; reword instead

## Level 3: Paragraphs

### One Idea Per Paragraph

Each paragraph makes exactly one claim, supports it, and interprets it. If you find yourself making two distinct points, split into two paragraphs.

### Topic Sentence First

The first sentence states the paragraph's claim. The reader should know what the paragraph is about before encountering any evidence.

### Evidence Sandwich: Claim -> Data -> Interpretation

1. **Claim**: State the finding or argument (topic sentence)
2. **Data**: Present the evidence (statistics, citations, examples)
3. **Interpretation**: Explain what the evidence means and why it matters

### Length: 4-8 Sentences

- No single-sentence paragraphs in journal articles
- No paragraphs longer than ~200 words; split if needed
- A paragraph of fewer than 3 sentences usually means the idea is underdeveloped or belongs elsewhere

### Logical Transitions, Not Mechanical Ones

Transitions should reflect the logical connection between paragraphs:
- "This contradicts..." (opposition)
- "One explanation is..." (inference)
- "A similar pattern emerged..." (parallel)
- "This raised the question of..." (extension)

Do NOT use mechanical connectors that add no meaning:
- "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally," (unless truly additive)
- "In the same vein," (vague)
- "Interestingly," (if interesting, show why)

### Last Sentence Bridges Forward

The final sentence of each paragraph should connect to the next paragraph's topic, creating a chain of reasoning rather than isolated blocks.

### A2. Topic Progression: Constant or Chained (Williams, *Style*)

Every paragraph flows in one of two progression patterns:
- **CONSTANT-TOPIC**: the same 2-3 subject characters recur across sentences ("Participants... They... The same participants...").
- **CHAINED-TOPIC**: the news at the end of sentence *n* becomes the grammatical opener of sentence *n*+1.

A paragraph that introduces a new abstract subject in every sentence reads as a list of disconnected claims — an AI tell that survives word-level scrubbing.

**Audit procedure:** list the grammatical subject of every sentence in the paragraph. The list must form a small recurring cast (constant) or a visible hand-off chain (chained). If it reads as four or five unrelated abstractions, rewrite.

**BEFORE** (a new abstract subject every sentence):
> Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use. Perceived usefulness shaped trust in the tool. Isolation drove help-seeking. Accessibility influenced adoption. Age also mattered.

Subject audit: *workload strain / perceived usefulness / isolation / accessibility / age* — five unrelated abstractions. A list wearing a paragraph's clothes.

**AFTER** (chained-topic):
> Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use. This use was heaviest among teachers who rated the tool as useful. That perceived usefulness went hand in hand with trusting the tool's suggestions. And that trust was the strongest correlate of opening the app a second week.

Subject audit: *workload strain -> this use -> perceived usefulness -> that trust* — each subject picks up the previous sentence's news.

### A3. No Naked Abstraction (concreteness norms: Brysbaert, Warriner, & Kuperman, 2014)

Every abstract claim gets a concrete companion within one sentence: a specific instance, a named study, a worked example, or a number. Concrete language is read faster, remembered better, and rated as more credible; an abstraction left standing alone gives the reader nothing to picture. This is also the constructive half of the 6.31 fix: assert what a thing IS and immediately show an instance of it.

**Audit procedure:** mark every sentence whose grammatical subject is an abstract noun (accessibility, integration, adoption, scalability). Each marked sentence needs a concrete companion — instance, named study, example, or number — within one sentence of it. Two or more consecutive naked abstractions = rewrite.

**BEFORE** (three naked abstractions):
> Accessibility remains a major barrier to early-career support. Mentoring gaps persist across districts. Digital tools may change this picture.

**AFTER** (each claim anchored to a number or named instance):
> Support for new teachers is scarce: in one national survey, 47% of first-year teachers were assigned no mentor in their first year (Smith, 2023). A mentoring app can close part of that gap: a single deployment served 30,000 teachers in its first month at near-zero marginal cost (Jones, 2024).

### Paragraph Shape Diversity

Use named shapes and vary them deliberately. In any section with 5+ paragraphs, use at least 3 different shapes. Never use the same shape more than twice consecutively. Tag each paragraph's shape in the Argument Map.

| Shape | Structure | When to Use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Claim-first** | Topic sentence → evidence → interpretation | Default for Results |
| **Evidence-first** | Data/observation → then the claim it supports | When the data is surprising or counterintuitive |
| **Counterargument-first** | "One might expect X. But..." → then your claim | When challenging prior literature or expectations |
| **Question-first** | Rhetorical question → then the answer | Sparingly, for key transitions (max 1 per section) |
| **Narrative** | Chronological sequence of events/logic | For Method, for historical context in Introduction |
| **Contrast** | X is true in domain A. In domain B, the picture changes. | For comparing across groups, contexts, or studies |

**Variation rules:**
- In any section with 5+ paragraphs, use at least 3 different shapes
- Never use the same shape more than twice consecutively
- If all paragraphs are Claim-first, the section will read like AI-generated

**Boundary note (2026-07-08 HARD BAN):** Counterargument-first and Contrast are PARAGRAPH-level turns — the argument turns across sentences, which is allowed (AGENT.md: turn at the ARGUMENT level). They do NOT license sentence-level antithesis ("not X but Y", "A, not B" — see 6.31). And "One might expect X" states a reader expectation, which is fine; the hedged "One might argue that..." framing stays banned per 6.16.

## Level 4: Sections

### Introduction: The Funnel

1. **Open with the phenomenon**, not a literature review (Bem, 2003). The first sentence should hook a psychologist who has never studied your topic.
2. Narrow from broad context to the specific gap your study fills
3. Review relevant literature as needed to set up the gap
4. State the gap explicitly: "No study has examined..."
5. End with your study's aims and hypotheses

### A6. Hook Menu + Title Craft

**Openings menu** — the first sentence earns the reader with one of four hooks:
1. **Concrete case**: a specific person, session, or scenario the reader can picture.
2. **Striking verified number**: a real, checkable figure that carries the tension on its own.
3. **Direct statement of the tension**: the intellectual problem, asserted plainly in one sentence.
4. **Named-example contrast**: two named, real examples whose difference IS the research question. This contrast lives at the ARGUMENT level, across sentences (allowed per the Level 3 boundary note); sentence-level antithesis stays banned per the 6.31 HARD BAN (2026-07-08) — give each named example its own positive declarative sentence.

**Title craft:** declarative statements of the finding beat question and pun titles. Question-mark titles drew more downloads and fewer citations (Jamali & Nikzad, 2011). Highly amusing titles received fewer citations (Sagi & Yechiam, 2008). Colon structure is fine. Keep the paper's key term early in the title, where search engines and skimming readers find it.

**BEFORE** (pun-question title; throat-clearing opening):
> Title: "Can Apps Coach? Exploring Digital Tools for New Teachers"
> Opening: "In recent years, mentoring apps have become increasingly prevalent in teacher onboarding. Their implications are far-reaching."

**AFTER** (declarative title with the key term first; verified-number hook):
> Title: "Workload Strain Is Associated with Adopting a Peer-Mentoring App Among First-Year Teachers"
> Opening: "Thirty-one percent of the first-year teachers we surveyed had used a peer-mentoring app in their first term, more than had been assigned a formal mentor in the same period."

### Method: Replication-Ready, Not a Tutorial

- Write enough for a competent researcher to replicate the study
- Chronological order within subsections
- Justify choices: why this sample, why these measures, why this analysis
- Do not explain what a t-test or ANOVA is

### Results: Narrative Prose with Embedded Stats

- **State the finding in English first, then the statistics** (Bem, 2003)
- Order by importance or by the study's conceptual logic, not by the order you ran the analyses
- Every paragraph opens with a descriptive label and analysis type tag: "(confirmatory H1)" or "(exploratory)"
- Report effect sizes with confidence intervals throughout
- Use prose, not bullet points. Results is a narrative section.

### Discussion: Specific to General

1. **Open with the main finding in plain language.** No statistics in the first sentence of the Discussion.
2. Each main paragraph: finding -> how it relates to prior literature -> theoretical implication
3. Do not simply restate Results with different words. Interpret.
4. Limitations: honest but brief. Acknowledge the most serious limitation first (usually cross-sectional design). Frame as opportunities.
5. End forward-looking: what should be studied next and why it matters

### Abstract: Flowing Prose

Problem -> Method -> Key Findings -> Conclusion. One paragraph. No bullet points. No section headers within. Write it last.

### Intellectual Altitude Scale

Every section must reach an appropriate intellectual altitude. Shallower paragraphs are incomplete.

| Level | Name | Description | Example |
|-------|------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | Fact | Single observation | "31% used the mentoring app" |
| 2 | Pattern | Grouped observations | "Adopters were younger, more overloaded, higher in perceived usefulness" |
| 3 | Mechanism | Why the pattern exists | "Workload strain creates diffuse pressure that drives help-seeking" |
| 4 | Theory | Framework connection | "This aligns with the cumulative-load account: strain builds across domains, and the whole load matters more than any single task" |
| 5 | New Question | What we still don't know | "Whether app use relieves or perpetuates the underlying workload strain" |

**Section targets:**

| Section | Minimum Altitude | Maximum Altitude | Notes |
|---------|-----------------|-----------------|-------|
| Results | 1-2 for findings | 3 for key interpretive moments | Don't over-interpret in Results |
| Discussion (main findings) | 3 | 5 | Must reach theory for every main finding |
| Discussion (limitations) | 2 | 4 | Specific + forward-looking |
| Introduction (opening) | 3-4 | 5 | Start with the intellectual debate |
| Introduction (closing/hypotheses) | 2-3 | 3 | Concrete, testable |

**Enforcement rule:** If a Discussion paragraph about a main finding stays at altitude 2 (just grouping findings or restating Results), it is not doing its job. Push it to altitude 4 (theory) or 5 (new question).

### 4-Layer Interpretation for Discussion

Every Discussion paragraph about a main finding must cover four layers:

1. **Meaning**: What the finding says in plain language
2. **Mechanism**: Why this happened (the psychological process)
3. **Theory**: Which framework explains it (with proper attribution)
4. **Action**: What someone should do differently (clinical implication OR research direction)

Not necessarily four separate sentences; the layers can be woven into natural prose. But if any layer is MISSING, the paragraph is incomplete. Layer 4 (Action) can be brief. This prevents the most common AI Discussion failure: restating Results with "consistent with [Author]" appended.

**BAD (altitude 2, missing layers 2-4):**
> Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use. This is consistent with prior research on job demands and help-seeking (Smith, 2023; Jones, 2024).

**GOOD (altitude 4, all layers; plain positive declaratives per the 6.31 HARD BAN):**
> [Meaning] Cumulative workload strain across task domains was associated with mentoring-app use, and each individual domain contributed only a small share of that signal. [Mechanism] This suggests that teachers turn to the app when strain accumulates across several domains at once and erodes their capacity to cope through conventional support. [Theory] The cumulative-load account (Brown, 2019) predicted exactly this: when demands pile up across many domains simultaneously, the resulting pressure differs in kind from single-domain overload. [Action] In practice, this implies that supports may need to address the pattern of global strain alongside any single targeted demand (e.g., grading load through workflow tools).

### Evidence Tier -> Permissible Language Mapping

Check EVERY causal claim against the study design. If the paper is cross-sectional, "predicted" is NOT acceptable.

| Study Design | Maximum Causal Language | Example |
|-------------|------------------------|---------|
| RCT / Experimental | "caused", "produced", "led to" | "The intervention reduced reported strain" |
| Longitudinal | "predicted", "preceded", "prospectively associated" | "Workload strain at T1 predicted mentoring-app use at T2" |
| Cross-sectional | "associated with", "correlated with", "related to" | "Workload strain was associated with mentoring-app use" |
| Qualitative | "described", "reported", "experienced as" | "Participants described their use as..." |
| Meta-analysis | "evidence suggests", "pooled estimates indicate" | "Meta-analytic evidence suggests..." |

## Level 5: Narrative

### One Coherent Story (Bem, 2003)

"Do not simply report every analysis you ran." The paper tells one story. Every section, paragraph, and sentence should serve that story. If a finding doesn't fit the narrative, move it to supplementary materials or omit it.

### Hourglass Structure

Broad (intro opening) -> Narrow (specific gap, method) -> Narrow (results) -> Broad (discussion, implications)

### No Metacomments (Bem, 2003)

Do not narrate the paper's structure to the reader:
- BAN: "As mentioned previously..."
- BAN: "Now I will discuss..."
- BAN: "Before turning to the results..."
- BAN: "As noted in the introduction..."
- BAN: "The next section describes..."

The reader can see the section headers. Just present the content.

### Bem's Deletion Test

For every sentence, ask: "If I remove this, does the meaning of the paragraph change?" If no, remove the sentence. Apply ruthlessly.

### Fair Treatment of Opposing Views (Bem, 2003)

Be measured, not aggressive. Acknowledge alternative interpretations honestly. Demolish straw men and you lose credibility; engage with the strongest version of the counterargument.

### Argument Map (Mandatory Before Any Prose)

Before writing any section, build an Argument Map. See `manuscript/argument-map-template.md` for the full template. Every paragraph requires:
- A stated **Claim** (what this paragraph argues)
- **Evidence** (what supports it)
- A **WHY HERE** justification (the logical connection to the previous paragraph)
- A **Reader State After** (what the reader now knows or believes)
- A **Kill Test** (what breaks if this paragraph is removed)

**Unacceptable WHY HERE answers:**
- "Because it's related to the topic"
- "Because this is where this topic goes"
- "Because the reader needs background"

**Acceptable WHY HERE answers:**
- "Because P3 needs the reader to already know X from P2"
- "Because the counterargument in P4 only makes sense after the evidence in P3"
- "Because without this paragraph, the jump from P1 to P3 would feel like a non sequitur"

If removing a paragraph breaks nothing, cut it. If it breaks something, state what.

### Echoing Rule

The last substantive sentence of the Discussion should resonate with the opening question or hook of the Introduction. Not repeat it -- answer it, reframe it, or show how the question has evolved through the paper's findings.

**Introduction opens**: "Can an app really mentor a new teacher?"
**Discussion closes**: "Our findings move that question forward: can the teachers who turn to an app for coaching find there what a mentor would have given?"

**Introduction opens**: "What drives a new teacher to seek support from an app?"
**Discussion closes**: "They are driven by cumulative strain that erodes their capacity to reach a human colleague, and the app is what remains."

(Both closing examples use plain positive form — an earlier version used the "not X, but Y" shape now banned by 6.31.)

**Rule:** The echo should be recognizable but transformed by the paper's findings. If the closing doesn't connect to the opening, the paper hasn't told one coherent story (Bem, 2003). Test: read the first and last sentences side by side. Does the last sentence feel like it could only exist BECAUSE of everything in between?

## Level 6: Anti-AI Verification (31 Checks)

AI tells are not just words. Structural and stylistic patterns are often more revealing. After generating ANY text, run these checks. Additionally, integrate the Per-Paragraph Anti-AI Check DURING writing (see end of this section). Checks 6.27-6.30 are derived from Kobak et al. (2025) corpus analysis of 15.1 million PubMed abstracts. Check 6.31 is the antithesis HARD BAN.

### Word-Level

**6.1 Banned Word Scan**
Zero HARD BAN words; SOFT BAN words within limits (1-2 per section max).

**6.2 Grammar Precision (Bem, 2003; APA 7th)**
- "compared with" not "compared to" (for statistical comparisons)
- "data are" not "data is"
- "because" not "since" (when expressing cause; "since" = time only)
- "although" not "while" (when expressing contrast; "while" = time only)
- "that" for restrictive clauses; "which" for non-restrictive
- "affect" (verb) vs. "effect" (noun, usually)
- "fewer" (countable) vs. "less" (continuous)

### Sentence-Level

**6.3 Sentence Length Variance**
Split text into sentences, count words per sentence, compute SD. Target: SD > 8. Compute CV = SD / Mean; target CV >= 0.40 (human range: 0.40-0.60; AI range: 0.25-0.35). AI tends toward uniform ~20-word sentences.

**6.4 Hedging Chains**
Max 1 hedge per claim. "Could potentially perhaps suggest" = AI. No sentence with 2+ hedging words unless genuinely warranted.

**6.5 Participial Phrase Endings**
No sentences ending with "...highlighting the importance of future research" or "...marking a significant advancement." Human writers don't end sentences this way.

**6.6 Empty Intensifier Stacking**
No "truly remarkable and deeply significant" or "profoundly important and incredibly insightful." Stacked intensifiers are AI tells.

### Paragraph-Level

**6.7 Transition Monotony**
No 3+ consecutive paragraphs starting with the same word or transition pattern.

**6.8 Formulaic Paragraph Structure**
No AI-tell pattern: topic sentence + exactly 3 supporting points + concluding restatement. This is the most recognizable AI paragraph shape. Human paragraphs vary: some have 2 points, some have 5, some start with evidence before the claim. No more than 1 in 5 paragraphs follows this template.

**6.9 Echo Conclusions**
No final sentence that restates the first sentence in different words. AI does this in every paragraph. Humans use the final sentence to bridge forward.

**6.10 List-of-Three Addiction**
AI loves producing exactly three examples, three reasons, three implications. Vary: sometimes give two, sometimes four, sometimes one strong example instead.

### Section-Level

**6.11 Sweeping Conclusions**
Scan for grandiose closing phrases: "the human condition", "in an increasingly digital world", "as technology continues to evolve", "the future of the field." These are AI closing-paragraph tells.

**6.12 Excessive Signposting**
No "First... Second... Third... Finally..." in every paragraph. Use it once per section at most. Vary with "Beyond this," "A separate concern," "The picture changes when..."

**6.13 Symmetric Structure**
AI loves mirror-image sections (3 paragraphs in Results, 3 matching paragraphs in Discussion). Human papers regroup findings thematically rather than mirroring Results order.

### Tone-Level

**6.14 False Balance**
AI hedges everything equally. Confirmatory findings should sound confident; only exploratory findings get hedged. Match epistemic language to analysis type.

**6.15 "Interestingly" Without Showing Why**
AI announces surprise; humans create it through juxtaposition. Don't write "Interestingly, X"; show why X is unexpected by contrasting it with what the reader would expect.

**6.16 Excessive Politeness**
No "It is worth considering that..." or "One might argue that..."; state it directly. These hedged framings signal AI deference rather than authorial confidence.

**6.17 Regression to the Mean**
Every paragraph should NOT sound the same: same energy, same length, same structure. Human writing has peaks and valleys. Some paragraphs are punchy; others are expansive. Vary deliberately.

### Structural-Level (New Checks)

**6.18 Redemption Arc**
- **Tell**: Every piece bends toward healing, resolution, or hope, even when data is ambiguous or findings are mixed.
- **Detection**: Does the paper's conclusion feel tidier than the findings warrant?
- **Fix**: Let ambiguous findings stay ambiguous. Not every paper has a tidy conclusion. Some findings are uncomfortable; let them be.

**6.19 Negative Parallelism**
- **Tell**: "Not X, not Y, but Z" structure used for dramatic reveal.
- **Detection**: Scan for sentences structured as "Not... not... but..." (One variant of the broader antithesis HARD BAN — see 6.31 for the full 8-pattern scan.)
- **Fix**: Rewrite as direct statement. Say what it IS, not what it isn't. The negative setup is an AI dramatic device.

**6.20 Upgrading Construction**
- **Tell**: "She wasn't just X -- she was Y" used to elevate a point.
- **Detection**: Scan for "not just X... Y" constructions. (One variant of the broader antithesis HARD BAN — see 6.31.)
- **Fix**: Delete the setup. State the Y directly. If Y is strong, it doesn't need the X as a springboard.

**6.21 Semantic Nonsense**
- **Tell**: Phrases that feel profound but collapse under logic. "The chaos of her early life never intended her to be: whole" -- does chaos have intentions?
- **Detection**: For every vivid or metaphorical phrase, ask: is this LITERALLY coherent?
- **Fix**: If not literally coherent, the profundity is fake. Rewrite with precision.

**6.22 Staccato Fragment Drama**
- **Tell**: "The gap-toothed smile. The unguarded wonder." Sentence fragments strung together for emotional impact.
- **Detection**: Scan for consecutive fragments (sentences without a main verb).
- **Fix**: Use sparingly (max once per section). NEVER in academic manuscripts. OK occasionally in opinion pieces or social posts.

**6.23 Near-Miss Idioms**
- **Tell**: Close but slightly wrong phrases. "Cameras stopped" instead of "stopped rolling." AI gets close to natural phrasing but misses the actual idiom.
- **Detection**: Read every idiom, metaphor, and colloquial phrase aloud.
- **Fix**: Verify it's the ACTUAL phrase native speakers use. When in doubt, restate literally.

**6.24 Even Distribution of Attention**
- **Tell**: Every finding gets equal Discussion space, every limitation equal length, every implication equal development.
- **Detection**: Does the Discussion give each finding roughly the same amount of space regardless of importance?
- **Fix**: Asymmetric treatment: main finding gets 2 paragraphs, secondary gets 1, tertiary gets a sentence. Mirror the importance hierarchy of the findings.

**6.25 Predictable Argument Structure**
- **Tell**: Setup -> complication -> resolution in EVERY section, every paragraph.
- **Detection**: Map the opening move of each paragraph; do they all follow the same arc?
- **Fix**: Vary deliberately: some sections open with the conclusion and work backward, some open with a counterargument, some open with raw evidence.

**6.26 Generic Applicability**
- **Tell**: A paragraph could appear in any paper on this broad topic with minimal changes.
- **Detection**: Ask: does this paragraph contain something SPECIFIC to THIS study's exact data, exact method, exact context?
- **Fix**: If you could copy-paste it into a different paper, it's generic. Add the specific detail, the exact number, the particular mechanism that is true only here.

**6.27 Marker Word Clustering (Kobak et al., 2025)**
- **Tell**: Three or more words from ANY of the Kobak lists (hard-ban, soft-ban, or frequency-flag) co-occurring within a single paragraph. Detection is statistical: individually fine words become AI fingerprints when clustered. The 10 most diagnostic common markers: across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within. But the full Kobak corpus contains 408 style words; ANY three co-occurring flags detection.
- **Detection**: After writing each paragraph, count ALL words that appear on any Kobak list (hard-ban + soft-ban + frequency-flag). Three or more from ANY list in one paragraph = red flag. Also check section-level frequency: any soft-banned word appearing 2+ times per section, or any frequency-flagged word appearing 3+ times per section.
- **Fix**: Replace most markers with plain equivalents. Keep at most one per paragraph. Distribute surviving markers across paragraphs rather than clustering. When replacing, prefer the simplest possible verb or adjective -- the kind a tired academic would reach for, not the kind that polishes the prose.

**6.28 Style-Verb Inflation (Kobak et al., 2025)**
- **Tell**: High ratio of ornamental verbs to informational verbs. In the Kobak corpus, 66% of excess AI style words were verbs -- making ornamental verb choice the single strongest AI fingerprint. Ornamental verbs include: enhancing, facilitating, illuminating, streamlining, showcasing, bolstering, garnering, orchestrating, unveiling, encompassing, harnessing, empowering, elevating, enabling, encapsulating, fostering, crafting, propelling, unlocking, unraveling, solidifying, pinpointing, revolutionizing, excelling, navigating, pioneering, culminating, spurred, evolving. Informational verbs include: measured, found, showed, differed, increased, predicted, reported, examined, tested, compared, assessed, observed, noted, used, included, reduced, improved.
- **Detection**: After writing a section, list all verbs. If more than 10% are ornamental style verbs, the section reads as AI-generated. Also check: does any verb feel like it was chosen to make the prose SOUND better rather than to describe what HAPPENED?
- **Fix**: Replace ornamental verbs with plain informational verbs. "Facilitates" becomes "helps." "Illuminates" becomes "shows." "Encompasses" becomes "includes." "Harnesses" becomes "uses." "Elevates" becomes "increases." "Empowers" becomes "enables" or "helps." The target: 95%+ of verbs should be ones a human researcher would use in a lab meeting.

**6.29 Adverb Inflation (Kobak et al., 2025)**
- **Tell**: Kobak identified 23 excess adverbs. AI overuses intensifying and qualifying adverbs: effectively, exceptionally, predominantly, meticulously, specifically, primarily, typically, ultimately, notably, additionally, subsequently, consequently, deductively, inadequately, initially, swiftly, detrimentally, conversely, seamlessly, intricately, particularly, predominantly, meticulously.
- **Detection**: Count adverbs ending in -ly per section. More than one Kobak-flagged adverb per 200 words is above human frequency. Also check for adverb stacking (two -ly adverbs within 3 sentences).
- **Fix**: Delete most adverbs. If the sentence means the same without the adverb, cut it. "Predominantly driven by" becomes "driven mainly by" or just "driven by."

**6.30 Adjective Inflation (Kobak et al., 2025)**
- **Tell**: Kobak identified 53 excess adjectives, many of which are evaluative rather than descriptive. Key offenders: comprehensive, crucial, compelling, diverse, distinct, exceptional, formidable, foundational, heightened, impactful, imperative, impressive, inherent, innovative, invaluable, meticulous, multifaceted, notable, nuanced, pivotal, pressing, prevalent, pronounced, remarkable, renowned, robust, seamless, substantial, thorough, transformative, uncharted, unexplored, unparalleled, valuable.
- **Detection**: For each adjective, ask: does this describe a measurable property, or does it evaluate/praise? "Large" is descriptive. "Remarkable" is evaluative. AI uses evaluative adjectives where humans use descriptive ones.
- **Fix**: Replace evaluative adjectives with descriptive ones. "Remarkable increase" becomes "large increase" or "threefold increase." "Comprehensive review" becomes "review of N studies" (let the number speak). "Crucial finding" becomes "key finding" or just state the finding without the adjective.

**6.31 Antithesis / Negation-Contrast Tic (HARD BAN)**
- **Tell**: Prose built on contrast. The #1 "reads like AI" tell; a fully-worked draft was rejected whose scan showed 24 such sentences in ~70 while every other check passed. Subsumes and generalizes 6.19 (negative parallelism) and 6.20 (upgrading construction).
- **Detection**: The 8 patterns measured by writer_lint.py's ANTITHESIS check (the canonical deterministic gate):
  (1) "not ... but" within one clause; (2) "X, not Y" appositive contrast (", not " — "not only/just" exempted by the regex but capped by pattern 8); (3) "rather than"; (4) "instead of"; (5) "is/are/was/were not ... it/they is/are" reframes; (6) "neither ... nor"; (7) "none of these/them" negate-then-assert doublets; (8) "not just". Also scan by eye for antithetical semicolons that balance two clauses for effect ("a list cannot warn about a transition; a space can") — banned in AGENT.md's HARD BAN though not yet a lint regex.
- **Cap**: the lint allows max(2, words/400) so 1-2 deliberate contrasts survive; the writing target is ~0. This is a tolerance for the unavoidable, never a budget to spend.
- **Fix**: State what a thing IS and give a concrete example. When tempted to write "not A but B," delete "not A" and assert B. Keep the full rule + before/after rewrites in `agent-memory/writer/feedback_no_antithesis_negation_sentences.md`.

### Per-Paragraph Anti-AI Check (During Writing, Not After)

The biggest shift in anti-AI practice. Instead of write -> check -> fix, ask these questions WHILE writing each paragraph:

1. Could this paragraph appear in any paper on this topic?
   If yes: rewrite with specifics from THIS study.
2. Does it follow the same shape as the previous paragraph?
   If yes: change shape.
3. Am I using the same sentence length as the last 3 sentences?
   If yes: vary (insert a short punch or a long elaboration).
4. Is the logical connection to the previous paragraph STATED or just IMPLIED?
   If implied and not obvious: make it explicit.
5. Would your advisor write a margin comment here?
   If the answer is anything other than "no": fix NOW, don't continue.
6. Is there a vivid phrase that sounds profound?
   Test: is it literally coherent? Could a skeptical reader challenge the logic?
7. Am I distributing attention evenly across findings?
   If yes: redistribute. More space for what matters more.
8. Does this paragraph contain 3+ Kobak marker words? Markers include: the 10 common set (across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within), ALL soft-banned words, ALL frequency-flagged words, and ANY word from the Kobak 408 style-word list. Three co-occurring in one paragraph = statistical red flag.
   If yes: replace most with plain equivalents. Keep at most one marker per paragraph.
9. Am I using ornamental style verbs? Check EVERY verb in the paragraph. Ornamental = chosen for polish rather than information. Key offenders: enhancing, facilitating, illuminating, showcasing, bolstering, encompassing, harnessing, empowering, elevating, enabling, crafting, propelling, unlocking, unraveling, solidifying, pinpointing, revolutionizing, pioneering, excelling, fostering, culminating, evolving, spurred.
   If yes: replace with informational verbs (shows, helps, includes, uses, found, tested, measured, reported).
10. Am I using evaluative adjectives where descriptive ones would work? Evaluative = praises or judges (remarkable, crucial, compelling, substantial, impressive, invaluable). Descriptive = measures or specifies (large, common, first, three-fold, 15%).
   If yes: replace with descriptive adjectives or let the data speak for itself.
11. Adverb check: does this paragraph contain more than one Kobak-flagged adverb (effectively, exceptionally, predominantly, meticulously, specifically, primarily, typically, ultimately, notably, additionally, subsequently, consequently, swiftly, seamlessly, intricately, particularly)?
   If yes: delete most. If the sentence means the same without the adverb, cut it.

### The Advisor Test

The most important single check. <ADVISOR> unknowingly reviewed AI-generated text and those comments produced Lessons 38-50 (now in `lessons/paragraph-level.md` and `lessons/argument-level.md`). Before finalizing any paragraph, imagine your advisor reading it with a red pen. The lessons tell you exactly what they catch:

- Synonym variation for the same concept (Lesson 42): one concept, one term
- Empty authoritative terms (Lesson 39): can the reader understand the term without reading its definition?
- Paragraphs without statable purpose (Lesson 47): can you state each paragraph's function in 5 words?
- Unnamed/unframed categories (Lesson 43): every table and list preceded by "what these are and why"
- Fabricated clinical interpretations (Lesson 45): every clinical claim traces to theory, not plausibility
- Vague quantifiers instead of numbers (Lesson 48): M, SD, n, scale -- always

If a paragraph would survive the advisor's red pen, it passes. If not, revise before moving on.
